## 1. What GOOD records (contract question 1)

**The literature**
- G12 pp.24–25: "verbs and adjectives of emotion and sensation are based on FEEL, using components like (for emotions) 'someone felt something (good/bad)'".
- GY14 p.6: emotion explications use "GOOD, BAD and VERY, to express the quality of the feeling".
- CH keeps the two uses apart: FEEL "someone feels something (good/bad)"; THINK "someone thinks something good/bad about someone else/something".
- W1999 p.56, on *pleasure*: "It is only the feeling which is (feels) 'good', no cognitive evaluation needs to be involved."
- W1999 p.97: "The feelings involved can be either 'good' (as in the case of gratitude and admiration) or 'bad' (as in the case of envy or contempt); and the evaluative components of the cognitive scenario are not always aligned in value with the feeling; e.g. in the case of envy 'something good' happens to another person, and yet one feels 'something bad', whereas in the case of Schadenfreude something bad happens to another person, and yet one feels 'something good'."
- **The quality can be left open.**
  - G15 p.5: "For some of the explications, the feeling quality is indicated as specifically 'good' or 'bad'. For example, with shocked the feeling is 'bad'." *shocked* "felt something bad" [D] (W1999 p.97 agrees); *surprised* [A] and *astonished* [C] "felt something". G15 p.16: "is this feeling characterised as good or as bad, or is it left unspecified?"
  - *concern*: "this person feels something", and the reason, in both W1990b p.373 and W1999 p.86: "concern is not necessarily a 'bad', undesirable feeling, whereas worry is (cf. 'this person feels something' vs. 'this person feels something bad')".
  - W1999 pp.44–45, on Russian *grust'*: "the explication invites the inference that the feeling caused by it is a 'bad' feeling, but the explication does not state this explicitly."
- *amazed* is "felt something good" (G15 [B], with evidence on p.7: "?I'm amazed at the rapid spread of the ebola virus" sounds insensitive). W1992 had said "concepts such as surprise or amazement do not imply anything 'good' or 'bad'", about the noun *amazement*. By the precedence rule, G15 wins.
- **Nouns carry the same slot.** W1999's nouns open "X felt [noun]" and end with the felt quality: *fear* "when this person thinks this this person feels something bad" (p.75); *sadness* "because of this, this person feels something bad" (p.39); *sorrow*, *grief* and *despair* "something very bad" (pp.67–70). W1990b does the same for *fear*, *fright*, *anguish*, *anxiety*, *apprehension* and *panic* ("bad") and *joy* ("very good"), pp.360–373.
- **Verbs carry it too.** Finnish *vihata* 'hate', in a definition by Vanhatalo and Goddard: "this someone Y is someone very bad / I want something bad to happen to this someone / ... because of this, this someone X feels something very bad towards this someone Y" (TVS19 §3.1). The judgement of a target is thought content (THINK, WANT), separate from the feeling.

**In GSE's cell states.** GOOD records the felt valence, the quality NSM writes as "feel something good/bad".
- It is +1, −1, or empty when the word leaves the quality open (*surprised*, *concerned*). Empty, not 0: the definition is silent about it (F118).
- It is read from the line that states the feeling. An evaluation inside the thought can have the opposite sign: *relieved* thinks "I thought that something bad would happen" and feels "something good" (W1999 p.58), and *disappointed* is its mirror (p.71). The coordinate keeps the felt valence, and the reading records a basis limitation with both roles intact (design §3).
- F066 holds: emotion words are signed, or left open; never 0.

**Consequences**
- **Rows 19–21 take GOOD from the certified pole when the reading carries a valence scale:** USAS E2 (liking), E3 (anger), E4.1 (happy/sad) or E4.2 (contentment).
- **E5 and E6 are not valence scales (revised).** The earlier draft gave E5− readings outside the Fear frame a typed GOOD field, because surprise sits on E5's minus side. E6's minus side fails the same way: it is USAS's apprehension side, and USAS tags *concerned* and *concern* E6−, whose feeling both Wierzbicka analyses leave open (above).
  - **The rule:** a reading whose slice tags are all E5− or E6−, and which has no Fear unit of its own part of speech, gets GOOD as a typed field: +1, −1 or empty.
  - **Reach:** 22 readings: 14 adjectives, 6 nouns, 2 verbs (`evidence/template_fields.json`, `class_membership`). Fear readings keep GOOD −1: every sourced Fear explication feels "something (very) bad".
  - **Controls:**
    - *concerned* and *concern* → empty;
    - *worried* → −1 (W1990b p.372; W1999 p.85), and *alarmed* → −1 (W1999 p.81);
    - *anguish* → −1 (W1990b p.371);
    - *horrified* and *petrified* → −1, "very bad" (W1999 pp.77, 79);
    - G15's *amazed* (+1), *shocked* (−1), *surprised* and *astonished* (empty), which are not in the population and run as fixtures.
- ***pity* contradicts itself.** W1999's explication (p.101) ends "when this person thinks this this person feels something", with no quality. The text before it says "the focus of pity, then, is on the other person ... and so the feeling is 'bad' rather than 'good'". *pity* is E4.1−, so its pole gives −1, agreeing with the prose and not with the explication. Its gold carries the code `SOURCE_CONFLICTS_WITH_ITSELF`.

**The conflict with F057.** F057 starts with nouns in general: "Can a noun have a sentiment weight to it? Of course not." Then it gives *hatred* as the example: "'Hatred' is a noun without value attached (e.g. the sentence 'the patient experiences an abnormal rise in BP when experiencing strong emotions, such as love or hatred' does not carry value judgement). As a verb? 'I hate you' carries implicit value judgement."
- **What the literature says.** Nearly every feeling noun it explicates states the felt valence (above: W1999 and W1990b, primary).
  - The exceptions leave the quality open rather than removing it, and for reasons that hold for the adjective too: *concern* is explicated as "X was concerned" (W1999 p.86), and *compassion* (p.103) and *pity*'s explication (p.101) have the same open line.
  - None is open because it is a noun. W1999 p.50 warns that "the nouns do not always mean the same as the corresponding adjectives", so where one explication covers both forms, the gold says which form it frames.
- **On *hatred* itself the evidence is now weaker.**
  - W1990b p.360, repeated in W1992, says anger's impulse to harm is held back "unlike the case of 'hatred'": anger is "'I WOULD want to do something bad to this person' (rather than 'I want')". Read with that contrast, hatred carries the experiencer's outright wish to harm the target.
  - W1999 p.88 then withdraws that component from anger: "Over the years, I have experimented with a number of different scenarios for anger myself, including components like ... 'I (would) want to do something bad to this person because of this' ... Upon further testing, however, it transpired that the more general form of the anger scenario outlined above is more accurate".
  - The aside about hatred is never retracted, but its point of comparison is gone.
- **What is unsourced.** No source explicates English *hatred*, *hate* or *love*. W1999 says so (pp.121–122): "Perhaps the most glaring omission is that of love ... For reasons of space, too, I have omitted hate, humiliation, exasperation, enthusiasm, regret, and many others". Finnish *viha* weakens the noun's judgement (TVS19 §3.3).
- **Where F057 still holds.** Your clinical sentence attributes no judgement to the speaker. The literature's components belong to the experiencer.
- **The ruling covers GOOD for every feeling noun in row 21:** 52 readings, including *hatred* and the four Fear nouns (*apprehension, dread, fear, terror*).
  - **Default:** the felt valence:
    - −1 for the 4 Fear nouns;
    - from the pole for the 42 nouns with a valence-scale tag (−1 for *hatred*);
    - the typed field for the 6 whose tags are all E5− or E6− outside Fear (*anguish, concern, desperation, dismay, horror, stupefaction*).
  - **Alternative**, F057 read literally: GOOD empty for every feeling noun. That empties it even where BAD sits in the thought content ("some bad things can happen" in *fear*), and the content is then recorded only in the derivation.
- **What doesn't change.** Adjectives, verbs and the fear-adjective target don't depend on it. Under the alternative, the 6 E5/E6 nouns have one field fewer. Your call.

## 2. What the substantive coordinates record (contract question 2)

**The literature**
- G12 p.16: "The frames define core argument structure, inherent aspect, causal notions, and the controlled nature of the activities." The emotion frame names its participants as slots: "this someone X thought something about something at that time / at the same time this someone felt something because of it" (G15 p.4).
- W1999's frames do the same: "sometimes a person thinks about something:" (*afraid* p.75, *dread* p.80), "about someone:" (*angry* with Y, p.88), "about someone else:" (*envy* p.98, *pity* p.101, *gratitude* p.105).
- GW2014 p.56, restating GW07 p.766: "From a semantic point of view, butterflies are a 'kind of living thing', and spoons a 'kind of thing made by people', whereas heavy things or hard things are not conceived of as 'kinds of things' at all. While categorization is based on the concept kind, 'qualification' is based on the conceptual prime like."
- GW2014 p.57: quality adjectives' explications "begin with the component 'this thing is like this:—', thereby portraying what follows as an attributed quality or property of the thing being described".
- GW2014 p.59: "If I say that the soup on the table in front of me is salty, I do not mean that it belongs to a category of salty soups. I am just saying what this particular soup is like."
- FBG22 p.270: "When the headword is human, it feels something, while when the headword is non-human, there is a human who feels something because they think about the entity denoted by the non-human headword."
- W1999 p.75: "Fear (X felt fear) (a) X felt something because X thought something".

**In GSE's cell states.** A substantive coordinate (I, YOU, SOMEONE, SOMETHING, PEOPLE, BODY, KIND, PART) records what the word itself denotes, the head of its explication. Every other person or thing in the frame or the scenario is an argument role in the derivation, never a cell.
- This is your F057 box test, and GW2014 draws the same line: nouns categorize, adjectives qualify.
- NSM classes SAME and OTHER as determiners, and KIND and PART as relational substantives (G12 p.3). SAME and OTHER follow the substantive they modify. They are written only when sameness or otherness is the word's own content (*same*, *other*, *different*).

**Consequences**
- **3b holds, and covers KIND and PART too.** An adjective's own reading heads nothing, so I, YOU, SOMEONE, PEOPLE, BODY, KIND and PART are 0, like SOMETHING in row 3.
- **Feeling nouns** name the "something" X felt: SOMETHING +1, the other substantives 0, KIND 0 (F007). No emotion-noun explication in the sources uses KIND.
- **Experiencer verbs** head no substantive, so all their substantive cells are 0.
- *pity*'s "someone else", *concern*'s "this person" and *mourning*'s "someone who has died" are roles.

## 3. The emotion template (rows 19–22)

**The literature.** The published wordings of the English template:

| Wording | Feeling linked to thought | Typicality line |
|---|---|---|
| Wierzbicka 1990b and 1992 (every explication) | "because of this, this person feels something bad" | "sometimes a person thinks something like this: ... X feels like this" |
| Wierzbicka 1999, chapter 2 | "X felt something because X thought something" ... "because X thought something like this"; *sad* (p.62) and *rage* (p.92) have neither, and *happy* brackets it (p.52) | "sometimes a person thinks: ... when this person thinks this this person feels something bad / X felt something like this" |
| Wierzbicka 1999, chapter 1 (*sadness*, p.39) | "because of this, this person feels something bad" | "sometimes a person thinks: ... X feels something like this" |
| Goddard 2015, Goddard & Ye 2014 (G15 [A]–[D], [H]; GY14 [A]) | "at the same time this someone felt something because of it" | "like people feel at many times when they think like this" |
| Goddard & Wierzbicka 2014, *happiness* as an emotion ([H], p.120) | none | "like people feel at many times when they think like this" |
| Goddard 2018's template, as applied in FBG22 pp.265–266 | "Because of this, this someone feels something bad at this time" | "like someone can feel when they think like this" |

- G15 p.10 calls its wording "a standard NSM format for 'emotion' meanings".
- W1990b p.361 explains the two links: "it is one thing to feel something BECAUSE of a particular thought, and another, to feel AS a person would who would be thinking that particular thought."
- **Why some words drop the link.** W1990b p.361: "one can say / I feel happy (sad) today - I don't know why. / but hardly: / I feel disappointed (relieved) today - I don't know why." W1999 pp.62–63 applies the same test to *sad*: "one can say: I feel sad today, I don't know why. For this reason, component (a) of the explication has been formulated as 'X felt something', not as 'X felt something because X thought something'". The same test puts *unhappy* (p.63) and *pleased* (p.55) on the other side.
- LIKE and WHEN are in every typicality line (WHEN through "sometimes" in the 1990 and 1992 wording). The quantifier ("sometimes", "at many times") and CAN ("someone can feel") are in some wordings only.

**In GSE's cell states (row 22)**
- **Template lines.** A template line writes only the primes that every published wording of it contains.
  - FEEL, THINK and LIKE are +1 for all three rows. One literal exception is recorded below (*frightened*), and *pleasure* falls outside the template (below).
  - WHEN is +1 for adjectives and nouns.
  - For verbs, WHEN stays a field.
    - English verbs are explicated only in the noun format, and those have WHEN: W1990b's *fear* covers "noun and verb" ("any differences between them are minor", p.363 n.2), and W1999's *dread* covers the verb's uses.
    - The one verb-specific template, Finnish *vihata* (TVS19 §3.1), has no typicality line.
  - QUANTITY and CAN stay fields.
- **BECAUSE, revised from the primary texts.** It is written only for a class in which every published wording links thought and feeling with it:
  - **Fear readings**, adjectives and nouns: all 8 sourced explications (W1990b pp.363–373; W1999 pp.75–86).
  - **E5/E6 readings outside Fear**: G15's frame; W1999's *petrified*, *horrified*, *alarmed*, *worried* and *concern*; W1990b's *anguish*.
  - **Other nouns**: every sourced slice noun (W1999's *sadness*, *sorrow*, *grief*, *despair*, *pity*). GW2014's *happiness* as an emotion has no BECAUSE at all, but *happiness* is not in the population. The noun rule must be re-checked if the population grows.
  - **Verbs**: W1990b's *fear* (noun and verb, p.363), W1999's *dread* (p.80), TVS19's *vihata*.
  - **Other adjectives: a field.**
    - W1999's "X was sad" has no link by design (the test above), and *happy* brackets it.
    - GW2014 p.121 (note 6) explicates the cooperative "I'm happy with (these arrangements ...)" with none either: "I can think about it like this: 'this is good' / when I think about it like this, I can feel something good".
    - The 58 other adjectives include the *sad*-type mood words (*glum*, *dejected*, *downcast*, *despondent*), and the template gives no warrant for them.
    - *sad*'s own field still finds BECAUSE, inside its thought: "I can't think: I will do something because of it now".
  - The earlier claim that "every later wording has the link" was wrong for *sad*. The latest direct English analysis of *sad* is W1999's, and it drops the link on purpose.
- **What the check shows.** Thirteen published explications are projected as gold (`evidence/template_fields.json`):
  - Every fixed cell agrees, with two exceptions:
    - *frightened*'s THINK: W1990b p.367 prints "sometimes a person **feels** something like this:" where every other explication in the paper prints "thinks". This was checked on the page image and is almost certainly a misprint; its code is `NOT_IN_EXPLICATION_SUSPECTED_MISPRINT`.
    - *pity*'s GOOD (§1).
  - INTENSITY comes from row 5's degree law where an explication has no VERY.
  - The fixed cells were drawn from these same explications, so this shows only that the rows don't contradict their sources. It is not a held-out test.

**A word the guards admit but the template does not fit: *pleasure*.**
- **The conflict.** F129: "Yes, emotion words incude the thinking part." W1999 p.56: pleasure "is usually not regarded as an 'emotion' at all, and with good reason, for it doesn't imply any cognitive scenario at all, not even a prototypical one. Rather, it implies only that a person feels something good because of something that is happening to him or her at the same time".

## 6. The four founder questions

These were posed at stage 3 in turn 2 of this session.

### "Should VerbNet's `cause` map to CAUSE or BECAUSE?" (a decision for you)

**What the literature settles: NSM's side only.**
- No CAUSE prime appears in CH, G12's Table 1 (p.3), G15's Table 1 or W1992's list.
- CH: "Don't include any complex English-specific words, no matter how common and seemingly basic, e.g. and, or, make."
- NSM writes causation as BECAUSE with DO or HAPPEN:
  - CH's BECAUSE box: "it happened because this someone did something before";
  - G12's frame for *eat*, *drink* (p.16): "because of this, something is happening to this something at the same time";
  - W1999's *guilt* (p.121): "I did something / something bad happened because of this";
  - W1999's *angry* at Y (p.89): "something bad happened / because someone did (didn't do) something".
- G15 p.13 frames the causer of an emotion as "something happened at this time / because of this, this someone X thought something about something at that time", adding: "Since the experiencer's thought is presented as being 'caused', the implication is that the experiencer had little or no control over it."

**Why it stays yours.** GSE's basis has both CAUSE and BECAUSE, and NSM has no CAUSE to tell us what yours is for. Design §7.3 expects two: "Native causation and explanatory BECAUSE likewise need their distinct source-to-coordinate contracts." No founder line rules on CAUSE. So the record already points to option B, and option A is the departure.

**Option B (recommended: it follows the design and loses no distinction)**
- **What CAUSE means.** The word's own causative event: a causer's DO or HAPPEN brings about a change in another participant.
- **What BECAUSE means.** A reason or thought-to-feeling link inside the meaning.
- **Row 25 becomes:** VerbNet's `cause(...)` writes CAUSE +1, with DO +1 for an agent causer and HAPPEN +1 for an event causer.
- **Row 26 is dropped.**
- **Witnesses:**
  - *kill*: CAUSE.
  - *frighten*: CAUSE for stimulus → experiencer, and BECAUSE for the experiencer's thought → feeling.
  - *afraid*: BECAUSE only. CAUSE stays a field for the emotion slice, until a law writes it.
- **The cost.** The gold needs a projection rule that sorts each NSM "because" by that structural test.

**Option A (NSM-literal)**
- **Row 25 as drafted:** `cause(...)` writes BECAUSE +1 with DO or HAPPEN.
- **Row 26:** CAUSE is empty in every reading under this basis version.
- **What it costs.** Causatives and emotion words share one mark, and GSE gives up a coordinate it built.
- **What it gains.** One fewer open field per word, and NSM explications project with no sorting.

Either way the fear target is unaffected beyond that one field (40 or 41).

### "What should function words emit?"

**The literature.** CH's function-word box:
- "'in', e.g. in this place; in one moment / 'at', e.g. at this time; at this moment / 'of', e.g. one of these people; something of one kind / 'with', e.g. do something with someone/something / 'about', e.g. think/say about something/someone";
- "'these': variant of 'this' used with a plural noun / 'it' (dummy subject), e.g. it is good if ..., it is bad if... / 'it' for 'this thing' / 'a lot': a variant of 'very much~many' / 'well': an adverbial variant of 'good' / 'such': portmanteau of 'like' and 'this' / 'they' or 'them' for 'these people' or 'these things' / 'everywhere': portmanteau based on 'all' and 'place' / 'often', 'sometimes', 'always', 'never': portmanteaus based on quantifiers and 'time'".

**In GSE's cell states**
- **Allolexes and portmanteaus emit the primes they stand for:**
  - *well* → GOOD;
  - *a lot* → QUANTITY, with INTENSITY for "very";
  - *such* → LIKE;
  - *everywhere* → QUANTITY and WHERE;
  - *often*, *sometimes*, *always*, *never* → QUANTITY and WHEN, told apart by NUMBER (F096). Whether *never* also takes NOT, the chart doesn't say.
- **Valency markers carry no sign of their own.** *in*, *at*, *of*, *with* and *about* each mark a slot of the prime they go with:
  - *in*, *at*: place or time;
  - *of*: kind, part or one;
  - *with*: DO's instrument or companion;
  - *about*: the topic of THINK or SAY.
- **Unmapped words get no lexical vector** and belong to GSE's grammar layer (design §1). These are *and* and *or*, which CH excludes from explications, plus *but*, *the* and *a*, which no source classifies. This is GSE's choice, not a sourced rule.
- **Two basis gaps show up:**
  - THIS has no GSE coordinate, so *this*, *these*, *it*, *they* and *such* can only be written in part.
  - GSE's WITH/WITHOUT has no NSM prime. NSM's *with* is a function word ("do something with someone", "someone is with someone else").

### "What do *box* and *dog* get?"

**The literature**
- GW2014 p.56 (restating GW07): nouns for kinds of things categorize. Spoons are "a 'kind of thing made by people'" and butterflies "a 'kind of living thing'".
- Kind nouns imply parts (GW2014 p.55): "the word elephant implies the presence of a trunk ... the word jug to a handle".
- G12 p.15: templates exist for "artefact and natural kind terms (Wierzbicka 1985)".

**In GSE's cell states.** Under §2, the head is the noun's own denotatum.
- *box* and *dog* get SOMETHING +1. A living kind also gives ALIVE +1, through the "kind of living thing" GW2014 uses for butterflies; for *dog* that is an inference, not a published explication.
- **KIND:** NSM puts KIND in every kind-noun explication ("of one kind"). F007 writes KIND +1 only when the sense denotes a category itself (*species*). F007 is approved and governs: *box* and *dog* get KIND 0, and the classifier stays a role in the derivation. This is a deliberate GSE departure from a literal projection.
- **The rest of their cells** come from the artefact and natural-kind templates (Wierzbicka 1985), which are still not among the sources. Reading them is the noun route's next step.

### "Does the GOOD prime itself carry INTENSITY?"

**The literature.** CH's VERY box: "very big, very small, very good, very bad, very far, very near, a very short time, a very long time / I want it very much (=very) / very very big, very very good, very very far etc." The LITTLE~FEW box adds "very little, very few". W1999's *apprehension* feels "something bad, not very bad" (p.86): the degree is relevant even when it is low.

**In GSE's cell states**
- **Now:** taking VERY is a combinatorial property, and design §2 puts those in the atom's profile. Record it on the atoms for GOOD, BAD, BIG, SMALL, FAR, NEAR, LONG_TIME, SHORT_TIME and WANT.
- **At stage 3 (recommended):** also write INTENSITY +1 on those atoms' vectors, through row 5's degree law, with the VERY box as the warrant that their own content is graded. Then the atom agrees with the built *good*, which already gets INTENSITY +1.
  - F126 ("intensity encodes that there is a measure of intensity that is relevant") supports it, and *apprehension*'s "not very bad" is an instance.
  - So does F088, which calls the one-cell atom "both true and false".
  - Design §2 asks that a second component on a declared atom be investigated rather than slipped in. Doing it as a stage-3 law change, with tests, meets that.
- **QUANTITY** merges NSM's MANY, FEW, SOME and ALL, and the chart grades only "few". It waits for the quantifier scale family (F096).
- Nothing in this pass depends on any of this.

## 7. Other open items the sources settle

- **Row 17, *hurry*:** "She hurried but didn't finish the report" is not a contradiction, so finishing is not part of *hurry*. That is a cancellation test, the standard check for an entailment. Restrict VN_END to VerbNet's aspectual classes (55.x).
- Rows 7 (Dimension) and 11 (*matter*) were already answered from their sources and are unchanged.

## 8. Recorded departures from NSM (not reopened)

- GOOD and BAD are one signed coordinate (F076). NSM keeps "not good" apart from "bad" (CH's NOT box: "not good, not bad").
- **GSE coordinates with no NSM prime:**
  - INFER, CAUSE (see §6), MUST, CERTAINTY, INTENSITY, START, END, TOWARD, AWAY, WITH and WITHOUT;
  - HAVE: G12's 2012 table has "HAVE (SOMETHING)", but CH v20 replaces it with "(IS) MINE" and "(HAVE) PARTS";
  - the poles FALSE and OUTSIDE.
  - EXIST corresponds to NSM's THERE IS, and BE to its two BE primes (G12 Table 1).
- **NSM primes with no GSE coordinate:** THIS and (FOR) SOME TIME.
  - VERY has none of its own; INTENSITY records whether degree applies (F125), which is a different contract.
  - How MORE relates to HIER_PLUS and HIER_MINUS is not established here.
- *the*, *a*, *and*, *or* and *but* get no lexical vector (§6).
